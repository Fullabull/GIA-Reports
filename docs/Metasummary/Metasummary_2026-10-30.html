Summary

Kim opens the October 30 report with Tank, returning the series to the GIA name, with a question that affects everyone: what do you really own? You work for a living, maybe buy a house and some land, and spend years paying for everything you need. But does that work make you wealthier, or does most of the wealth you create end up belonging to someone else?

Start with the word asset. An asset is simply something that produces value: a farm produces food, a factory produces goods, and people produce value through their work. That makes you an asset, too. Governments once worked more like employee-owned companies, where citizens held a stake in their country's resources, such as the minerals beneath the land, and the wealth those resources produced went to the people. Today, much of that wealth ends up on someone else's books.

Now consider your paycheck. In a Natural world, work would earn you something of real value. But our financial system runs on debt, and even the money you are paid is part of it. You work, get paid, pay taxes, buy what you need, and watch most of it disappear -- Kim estimates people keep less than 10-15% of what they make. The rest flows back into the system.

And the cycle keeps going. Products are designed to wear out so you have to replace them, and the constant pressure to buy the next thing is what Kim calls "living in lack." Illness keeps patients and their families paying for care year after year, because conditions are managed, not cured.

So where does the value you produce actually go? Kim follows the money through bonds. A bond is a debt that someone else can buy and collect interest on. Take a mortgage: a bank bundles 100 home loans in the MERS system, grades each borrower by risk, and turns the bundle into a bond. That bond is sold to the Fed or to private firms such as BlackRock -- so the bondholder, not the homeowner, ends up owning the deed. This is privatization, and since 1975 it has also moved government services such as Child Protective Services into private hands. You carry a bond of your own, too: your birth certificate bond, and every lender, even your employer, owns a piece of it. All of these bonds end up at ANNA, a numbering agency in Belgium, where new bonds are created in the name of preserving life -- the job of the Primordials, the first-created beings. That ended in 2007, and the markets crashed in 2008. Now broke governments squeeze new bonds out of whatever they can: traffic tickets, fines on land, privatized prisons, even criminal indictments.

Prisons lead Kim to the biggest bond of all, because all of us are prisoners. Humanity was captured by the Machine, whose bloodline is the fallen Primordials, and is held in a prison called the Matrix. It is a debtors' prison: in a debtors' prison you own nothing, and everything is taken from you the moment you are imprisoned -- which here means the moment you are born. The International Court of Justice enforces it, and the Elite run a private version of that court whose staff are the prison guards; their business cards even say they "serve the Nephilim." Because the debt can never be paid off, you come back to keep paying -- that is incarnation. The bonds issued against every one of us are the prisoner bonds, and they hold up the entire Financial System. The same hidden terms in birth certificates and mortgages have also let wars and bombings swap people out for cloned, soulless replacements (not the original people). Without new prisoner bonds, the treasuries, the Fed, and BlackRock cannot issue bonds at all -- and there is no time jump left to reset the system.

That is why governments are scrambling right now. Emergency meetings are under way, the Fed is counting on the Treasury to fix the problem, and the Treasury is trying to tap the prisoner bonds held at ANNA and use them in the crypto markets to prop up stablecoin. The reason is what happened last weekend: the "time of assessment," a review at which every Primordial went back to Source. They all failed. Source took away their authority to create and has made other arrangements for Creation. Kim had been watching Deep State operatives who gathered early in meetings beneath New York; it turns out they were waiting to be handed new jobs as the Primordials' Nephilim. The loss reaches the Machine too: its power -- really its authority -- came from the Primordials, and without them no one can give it any more. One declaration ending the prisoner bonds, and the whole market falls. The people you fear have lost, and now they want help from you.

Kim closes by asking who wants another billion years in debtors' prison, or to support the Deep State, the ICC, the International Court of Justice, or the World Health Organization: "I wonder who's in debt now?" More comes in the next report, and the GIA is back as a weekly series.
