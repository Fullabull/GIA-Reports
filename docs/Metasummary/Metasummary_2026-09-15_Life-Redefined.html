Summary

Kim opens the September 15, 2026 Life Redefined report by introducing Andrew Millison, a permaculture educator and filmmaker who has spent more than 30 years studying how damaged landscapes can recover. Millison traces his path from landscape and gray-water design to teaching at Oregon State University and eventually filmmaking, which he describes as "broad-scale teaching." He argues that the technical solutions already exist and that the greater obstacle is human will. His breakout came in India, where he documented the Paani Foundation's Water Cup Competition, which helped thousands of villages recharge their groundwater, and his film on the UN World Food Programme's Great Green Wall work in Senegal has surpassed 15 million views. His work now spans climate zones and cultures, including typhoon resilience in the Philippines, Mexico City's chinampas, Hawaii's gravity-fed ahupua'a taro terraces, and an Arizona neighborhood where water harvester Brad Lancaster cuts curbs and reshapes streets to "plant the rain."

Millison's central argument is that restoring hydrologic cycles is foundational: when water is slowed, captured, and returned to the land, forests, soils, biodiversity, food systems, and local resilience can recover with it. He describes restored ecosystems as "shock absorbers" for climate extremes and emphasizes that this work is already being done, from individual neighborhoods to entire regions. Kim closes by tying the examples together: water, soil, food, climate, and community are not separate problems, but parts of one connected living system.

Phrases And Concepts

  • ahupua'a -- Hawaii's traditional food system, where water flowed through terraced rock-wall gardens, fish ponds, and productive forests; built on communal land tenure and equal access to fresh water; its remains are being restored from beneath invasive forest.
  • chicane (curb extension) -- street-side water-harvesting basin that narrows the street to slow traffic, captures well over 5,000 gallons of water a year, and overflows into the next basin down the street.
  • chinampas -- Mexico City's system for farming low, flooded wetland areas; Millison's example for that landform.
  • curb cuts -- holes cut into city curbs so stormwater enters street-side basins instead of the storm drain; begun illegally in the Arizona neighborhood.
  • Earthships -- environmental architecture of northern New Mexico that drew Millison toward ecological design.
  • gray water -- mildly polluted water from showers and laundry; an Arizona reuse-law change led Millison to start a permaculture-based gray-water business.
  • Great Green Wall of Africa -- project to plant a barrier of trees across Africa from Senegal to Djibouti to stop the Sahara's southern expansion; a vegetated Senegal River zone could be its first line of defense.
  • "How the UN is Holding Back the Sahara Desert" -- Millison's video on a UN World Food Programme project in Senegal; over 15 million views.
  • hydrology of the planet -- stabilizing how water moves through ecosystems; Millison's central thesis and the basis for regrowing forests, restoring soils, and rebuilding biodiversity and biomass.
  • kalo (taro) -- starchy root that needs wet soil; grown in flooded rock terraces in the ahupua'a.
  • kuleana -- Hawaiian for responsibility; in the ahupua'a, whatever you did affected everyone below you.
  • Life Redefined -- Kim's series that sits down with visionaries, healers, farmers, inventors, and scientists to find solutions.
  • lo'i -- flooded taro patch fed by gravity from the stream; the inlet and exit sit on opposite sides so water moves evenly, then overflows to the next patch without erosion.
  • neighborhood rain-irrigated food forestry -- street trees on public commons watered by street runoff that shade and cool the street and feed residents.
  • permaculture -- holistic design system covering housing, agriculture, water, and energy; a roadmap for living in a way that increases water, soil, and resources rather than depleting them.
  • "planting the rain" -- Brad Lancaster's principle of shaping the land to capture rain before planting anything, so rain becomes the sole irrigation source.
  • shock absorbers -- Millison's term for restored ecosystems that buffer climate extremes.
  • tropical cyclones -- hurricanes and typhoons, the same storm named by region; Millison's current stretch documents resilience to their massive rainfall events.
  • Water Cup Competition -- Paani Foundation competition through which thousands of villages solved their water problems; regenerating water tables at a scale and speed Millison has seen nowhere else.
