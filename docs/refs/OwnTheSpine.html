UNN Architecture: Own the Spine, Rent the Limbs

A vendor-independent starting point for a custom UNN front end and back end

The architectural premise

The spine is the set of facts and rules that should remain true about UNN no matter which vendors UNN uses.

A useful test is:

If every vendor account disappeared tomorrow, what would UNN need in order to rebuild without losing its members, business rules, subscription relationships, content identity, and control?

Those durable facts belong in the UNN-owned spine. Video delivery, email delivery, storage, CDN, search, and other specialized services can then be rented as replaceable limbs.

The durable UNN spine

1. Identity

Who is this person?

UNN should have its own durable member ID that does not change when a vendor changes.

2. Billing relationship

What financial relationship belongs to this member?

UNN should preserve the billing provider and that provider's customer/subscription identifiers.

3. Membership state

What is this member's current relationship with UNN?

Free, trialing, paid, past due, canceled, comped, founding member, suspended, etc.

4. Entitlement policy

What is this member allowed to do right now, and why?

This logic belongs to UNN, not to a video vendor or CRM.

5. Content catalog

What content exists?

UNN owns the content ID, title, publication date, access class, master location, and delivery-provider mappings.

6. Audit / business events

What important state changes happened?

Joined, paid, failed payment, canceled, granted access, revoked access, published, suspended, restored.

Entitlement is UNN-owned state plus UNN-owned policy

It is tempting to reduce entitlement to a single stored field such as tier = premium. It is also tempting to reduce it to one giant function. Neither is quite right.

UNN-owned facts/state + UNN-owned policy rules = current entitlement decision

A conceptual policy contract might be:

can_access(member, content, now) -> { allowed, reason }

The function evaluates stored facts such as:

membership statustrial datesbilling statecontent publish datespecial grantsgrace periodssuspensions

The important point is ownership: Kim can change the business rule without requiring the video vendor, payment provider, or front end to define what the new rule means.

Examples of rules the spine should be able to express

Billing-provider independence

Do not assume Stripe. Stripe is a useful example because it is common and API-friendly, but the architecture should remain correct if UNN uses another processor today or changes processors later.

The billing provider should be treated as the authority for financial facts:

  • payment succeeded or failed
  • subscription started, renewed, changed, or canceled
  • current billing period
  • amount/currency
  • refunds/chargebacks where relevant

UNN should preserve enough information to reconnect those facts to its own member record:

billing_provider provider_customer_id provider_subscription_id

The billing provider owns payment truth. UNN owns membership truth.
A payment failure is a financial fact. Whether that means immediate downgrade, a 7-day grace period, limited free access, or something else is a UNN business rule.

One member can have more than one billing relationship

Keeping the provider separate from the member also avoids painting UNN into a corner during migrations or international expansion.

UNN member | +-- billing account: Provider A | +-- subscription 123 | +-- billing account: Provider B +-- subscription 456

The design does not need to assume this will happen. It simply should not make it impossible.

The request flow: where "headless" becomes real

1. Viewer clicks content on UNN's own front end. 2. Front end asks UNN's API: GET /api/access/{content_id} 3. UNN API: a. identifies the UNN member b. loads current membership/billing state c. loads the UNN content record d. evaluates UNN entitlement policy 4. If denied: return reason such as: upgrade / wait / past_due / suspended 5. If allowed: choose the current delivery provider mint the provider-specific short-lived playback credential return playback information to the front end 6. The video provider streams bytes. It does not decide what "Premium UNN Member" means.

The provider adapter is the swap boundary. Changing video providers should require an infrastructure migration and provider-specific integration work, but it should not require redesigning UNN's member model or business rules.

Provider independence does not mean "change one column"

A clean abstraction can prevent a vendor change from becoming a redesign of UNN, but the actual migration may still involve:

  • moving source assets or creating new delivery copies
  • new asset/playback IDs
  • captions and thumbnails
  • signed-token implementation differences
  • live ingest configuration
  • analytics integration
  • recording behavior
  • testing player/device compatibility

The goal is not to pretend migrations disappear. The goal is to make them infrastructure migrations rather than business-model migrations.

Why special entitlement grants deserve their own concept

UNN should not have to invent fake membership tiers every time Kim wants an exception.

  • founding-member access
  • complimentary access
  • temporary event access
  • extended grace periods
  • staff/moderator privileges
  • manual support corrections

This lets exceptions stay explicit instead of corrupting the underlying subscription record.

Community is the genuinely hard limb

Video delivery is relatively easy to abstract. Community is not.

  • posts and comments
  • direct messages
  • groups/channels
  • moderation history
  • member relationships
  • notifications
  • attachments
  • search indexes
  • blocks/reports/abuse state

If UNN rents this layer, that may become its largest remaining source of lock-in. If UNN builds it, it becomes a substantial software product in its own right.

Community deserves its own architectural decision. It should not be casually treated as another interchangeable utility.

Vendor termination risk belongs in the foundation

Exit planning usually asks: "What if UNN wants to leave?"

A more severe question is: "What if the vendor decides UNN has to leave?"

For every important dependency, UNN should understand:

  • whether its intended use is allowed by the vendor's terms
  • what data/assets can be exported
  • how much notice termination may provide
  • whether service can be suspended before export is complete
  • whether an alternate provider or recovery path exists
  • what must be independently backed up before a problem occurs

That analysis should apply not only to streaming, but also to billing, email, identity/authentication, hosting, storage, DNS/CDN, app stores, and any rented community layer.

Own the rules; rent the machinery

UNN-owned spine Identity Billing links Membership state Entitlement policy Content catalog Audit/business events Administrative control | | | v v v Video Email Other services provider provider / community

Each rented service should receive only the information it needs to do its job. The service should not become the place where UNN's business meaning lives.

Administrative control is part of the spine too

UNN staff cannot operate the system by editing database rows or issuing API calls manually.

  • find a member
  • see why access was allowed or denied
  • grant/revoke temporary access
  • inspect billing/membership state
  • correct support issues safely
  • suspend or restore accounts
  • view important audit history

This is part of the real product, not an afterthought.

Do not confuse conceptual simplicity with production scope

The core idea can be demonstrated with a small proof of concept. A production UNN backend must additionally consider authentication, account recovery, webhook reliability, retries/idempotency, staff permissions, refunds/chargebacks, monitoring, backups, security, privacy/deletion requirements, disaster recovery, and operational support.

No implementation-time estimate belongs in this architecture brief. The production scope should be estimated by the technical team responsible for building and operating it.

Before choosing more vendors, write Kim's business rules in plain English.

Every answer becomes a test case for the entitlement model. If the architecture can express Kim's rules cleanly without changing vendors, the spine is doing its job.

Where the streaming comparison now belongs

Cloudflare Stream, Mux, Amazon IVS, Bunny/Nimble and other delivery options are still important. They are simply a downstream infrastructure decision once the UNN-owned spine is defined.

The streaming comparison should therefore be treated as an appendix or implementation-options document rather than the architectural foundation.
